# 01: How to learn programming

## Why this matters

Most people who give up on programming don't lack talent. They lack a **method**. They watch tutorials, feel productive, then freeze in front of an empty file. This lesson gives you a method that works.

## Key ideas

### 1. Doing beats watching

You learn programming the way you learn to swim: in the water. Reading and watching build *recognition* ("I've seen this before"). Only writing code builds *recall* ("I can produce this myself"). Aim for at least **70% of your study time with your hands on the keyboard**.

> The "tutorial trap": you follow along, everything works, and you understand every line. Then you try to build something alone and can't start. The fix: after every tutorial or lesson, rebuild what you learned **without looking**, and then change it.

### 2. Deliberate practice

Practice that improves you has four parts:

1. **A specific goal**: "write a function that counts vowels", not "practice Python".
2. **Slightly too hard**: if it's easy, you're not learning. If it's impossible, shrink it.
3. **Fast feedback**: run your code often. Let errors, tests and reviews tell you what's wrong.
4. **Reflection**: after solving it, ask yourself what was hard and what you'd do differently.

### 3. Being stuck is the job

Professional developers are stuck most of the day. The skill isn't avoiding it, it's having a process:

1. **Read the error message.** Really read it, all of it. It usually tells you the file, the line and the problem.
2. **Say what you expected vs. what happened.** Write it down if needed.
3. **Shrink the problem.** Remove code until the bug disappears, then add it back piece by piece.
4. **Search** the exact error message.
5. **Ask for a hint** (`/hint`), not for the answer.
6. **Take a break.** Seriously: your brain keeps working on it.

Give yourself **20–30 minutes** of honest struggle before asking for a hint. That struggle is where learning happens.

### 4. Consistency over intensity

Your brain consolidates skills during rest. **45 minutes every day beats 6 hours on Sunday.** Pick fixed slots in your week and protect them like appointments.

### 5. Spaced repetition and retrieval

- **Retrieval**: test yourself before re-reading. `/quiz` exists for this.
- **Spacing**: revisit older topics after a few days, then a week. Each level builds on the previous one, so this happens naturally if you don't skip.

### 6. Keep notes in your own words

After each lesson, write 3–5 bullet points in `learner/level-NN/notes.md`: what you learned, what confused you, a question you still have. If you can't summarise it, you haven't understood it yet.

## Exercise

1. Look at your week. Choose study slots that add up to your `weekly_hours`. Be realistic.
2. Write your plan as a small table (day, time, duration). You'll need it for the project.
3. Write down one thing that made you quit learning something in the past, and what you'll do differently this time.

## Check your understanding

1. What's the difference between *recognition* and *recall*, and why does it matter for programming?
2. You've been stuck on an error for 10 minutes. What are your next three steps?
3. Why is studying 45 minutes a day better than 5 hours once a week?
